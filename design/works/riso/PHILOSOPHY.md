# Overprint Honesty

A movement that trusts the press more than the screen. Colour is not chosen; it is earned where two
transparent films of ink happen to land on the same fibres. A composition begins as a handful of
flat, cut shapes, each belonging to exactly one drum, and the picture only exists after they have
been pressed onto paper one after another. The overlap is the event. Everything else is setting
the stage for it.

Space is white paper, and white paper is never a background colour: it is the brightest ink
in the work, the thing every other ink subtracts from. Forms are few and large, cut with the
blunt confidence of scissors through card: a full disc, an arch, a heavy glyph, a band laid
slightly off square. They are placed so that each pair of inks meets at least once and each meeting
is big enough to read as its own colour field, never as a thin decorative fringe.

The surface carries the evidence of the machine, and that evidence is generated, never pasted.
Solids are mottled where the stencil starved, speckled where a fibre refused the ink, banded where
the drum turned a little unevenly, and every plate sits a fraction of a millimetre away from where
the file said it should. These are not filters laid over a clean image; they are the image. The work
must look as if a printer who has pulled ten thousand sheets chose this one from the stack because
its faults agree with each other: meticulously tuned, painstakingly calibrated, nothing accidental
left unexamined.

Order matters and is shown. The last drum sits on top and quietly wins its overlaps; turn the
order around and the same shapes produce a different sheet. A body of work in this language is
therefore a set of siblings: the same plates, pulled in different orders, at different densities,
compared side by side like specimens.

Text is set as if it were another plate: one heavy face, few words, printed in the same ink and
with the same faults as the shapes. Small marks of the trade (crop marks, registration targets,
a colour strip, a slug line with the pull number) are the only labels allowed, and they are
placed with the precision of master-level prepress, at the edges where a printer would expect them.

The final artefact should feel like a press proof pinned to a studio wall after countless hours of
adjustment: bright, flat, tactile, and exact about its own imprecision.
