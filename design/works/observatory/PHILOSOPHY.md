# Torchlit Cartography

A movement for images made to be read in the dark. Its first rule is subtraction: whatever light
a picture gives off is a cost to the eye that reads it, so every mark must earn its photons.
The ground is true black, not a tinted near-black, because the darkness is the subject and the
material at once. Marks sit on it the way ink sits on a chart held under a red torch: flat,
opaque, exactly where they are, never spilling past their edge.

Colour is a single filter, not a palette. Everything is one red seen at three densities: the
full lamp for what must be read, a darker gel for what only has to be sensed, and an almost-black
ember for planes that hold other things. There are no gradients, halos or soft edges, because a
filter does not invent light, it only removes. Brightness is expressed by size and by order of
stacking, the way a printed star atlas makes a first-magnitude star a larger dot rather than a
brighter one, with a knock-out ring cut around it so it can be told from its neighbours.

Space is circular and nested. The strongest compositions in this language are made of rings
inside rings: a disc that turns, a plate that stays, a window cut through the plate, scales
printed where one edge meets another. Meaning lives in alignment, in the instant one tick on a
moving ring meets one tick on a fixed ring. Composition therefore favours a single great circle
pushed off-centre and cropped by the frame, so the eye senses rotation even in a still.

Scale and rhythm come from counting. Day ticks, hour ticks, degree ticks, contour lines, the
stipple of a galaxy plotted dot by dot: repetition at many densities, laid down with the
patience of a draughtsman inking a scale by hand. The work must look meticulously crafted,
the product of deep expertise in measurement, every tick at its true angle, every gap between
a constellation line and its star the same width. Nothing approximate, nothing decorative.

Text is a label on an instrument. It is small, set in a plain coding face for numbers and a
calm serif for names, placed along the curves or in the margins where an engraver would put
it, and never larger than it needs to be. A single title may be large, but it stays the same
flat red, and it never competes with the circle. Master-level execution here means restraint:
the finished piece should feel like an object someone has used for years in the dark, labored
over until nothing could be removed, and still be legible to a dark-adapted eye at arm's length.
