# Sounding Lines

*A visual philosophy for 물길's portraits, mark and key visuals (canvas-design pass).*

Sounding Lines treats every subject the way a survey vessel treats the seabed: not as a picture to be
taken but as a surface to be measured, one pass at a time. Form is never drawn as an outline. It appears
only where a field of parallel tracks thickens, swells and bends as it crosses something solid — the way
an echo sounder reveals a reef by the change in its trace, never by a contour someone sketched. The
viewer assembles the figure from accumulated evidence, and the work rewards the second and third look.

Space is horizontal and patient. Lines run edge to edge at a fixed interval, like survey lanes, and that
interval is sacred: it is the grid of the discipline, the proof that the measurement was systematic.
Composition comes from what the tracks encounter, not from where they are placed. Generous margins of
untouched, evenly ruled field surround each subject, so presence is defined against quiet. Every
spacing, every weight transition, every taper at a line's end must look meticulously calibrated — the
product of painstaking, master-level care, as if each lane had been run and checked by hand.

Colour is the colour of the chart room: deep navy ink on a pale sea-white ground, a thin field of the
palest shoal blue. A single vermilion-orange line appears once per composition, always horizontal, always
at the same height across a series — the waterline. It is the only warm mark and it carries the whole
system's meaning: everything above it is measured from the surface. No gradients, no glow, no texture
that did not come from the measuring itself.

Scale and rhythm come from modulation, not from size. A line is thin where it crosses open water and
heavy where it crosses mass; the transition is smooth, eased, never stepped. Slight undulation — the
swell under the hull — runs through every trace so nothing is mechanically straight, yet nothing wanders.
Repetition builds density and density builds form: the craftsmanship lies in tuning those two forces
until a face, a shoulder or an island emerges unmistakably, without one stroke of illustration.

Text, when present, is clinical and sparse: a coordinate, a reference number, a name set small and
calm in the corner like a plate label in a survey report. It never explains. The hierarchy is always
the measured field first, the subject second, the label a distant third. The finished work should feel
like a plate from an imaginary hydrographic atlas — an artifact that took countless hours of exacting
observation, proving that something as unmeasurable as a person can be sounded, lane by lane, with
reverence and precision.
