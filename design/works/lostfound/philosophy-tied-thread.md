# Tied Thread

A movement about the moment a loose thing is joined to the one it belongs to. Its whole world is
three materials: a cool sheet of steel, white card, and a black line drawn by hand. Nothing else is
allowed in until a match is proven, and then one colour, a flat fluorescent green, arrives as a
verdict rather than a decoration.

Space is organised like a wall of lockers: a quiet field of identical doors, seams and vent slots
repeating with patient regularity, so that anything placed in front of it reads as an event. Forms
are cut, not drawn: card with two clipped corners and a punched eyelet, ticket paper torn along a
zig-zag, a metal clip. Every edge is the edge a real object would have, set down with painstaking
attention to how paper rests on a shelf, a two-pixel shadow and no more.

The line is the protagonist. A string leaves an eyelet, sags under its own weight, and lands exactly
on a clip; its curve is computed, not guessed, and it must look as if a careful hand laid it there.
Lines may cross, because real strings do, but they never tangle, and the eye can always follow one
from end to end. Composition is a ledger of these lines: left column of things, right column of
people, the gap between them the place where meaning is made.

Marks are written, never typeset, wherever a person would have written them: numbers circled in
marker, an X that is two strokes, an O that does not quite close. Printed type is reserved for what
the institution says. Text stays sparse and plain; the drawing and the string carry the story.

The work should look meticulously crafted, the product of deep expertise and countless small
refinements: alignments checked twice, sags tuned to the distance they span, the single green kept
for the one place it has been earned. Restraint is the craft. If an element does not help a thing
find its owner, it is removed.
