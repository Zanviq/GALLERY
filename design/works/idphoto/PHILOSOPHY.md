# Measured Likeness

A face, held still for a fiftieth of a second, is turned into a rectangle with rules. The movement
starts there: a likeness is not flattered, it is measured. Every mark in the work is either the
thing being measured or the instrument doing the measuring, and nothing else is allowed into the
frame. The result should read as the product of deep expertise, as if someone had framed ten
thousand faces and drawn the guide for the ten-thousand-and-first with the same painstaking care.

Space is a backdrop. One pale field of blue, flat and evenly lit, fills the ground the way a paper
roll fills the wall behind a stool; where the field ends, darkness begins, and that edge is the
only drama the composition permits. Form sits inside the field in strict frontality: one figure,
shoulders level, eyes on a line, centred to the millimetre. Symmetry is not a style here, it is
the requirement, and the composition earns its tension from how close everything comes to it.

Colour is three things and a fourth that obeys them: the blue of the roll, the white of the flash
and of the print paper, a cool near-black for hair, cloth and ink, and one deeper blue lifted from
the backdrop for the instrument alone (bands, rulers, crop corners). No warmth enters. Tone comes
from density of line and from the paper itself, never from added hues. Each value is calibrated
with master-level restraint so the figure reads at a thumbnail and the rulers read at full size.

Scale and rhythm come from repetition. The same likeness appears eight times on a 4×6 sheet, then
again cut apart, each cut separated by a dashed trim line and a hair of white. Repetition is the
proof: the eye checks the second copy against the first and finds them identical, which is the
point of the whole trade. Rulers tick in millimetres, bands hold tolerances, a single dimension
line states a number. These systematic marks accumulate quietly until the picture becomes a
specimen sheet from a discipline of its own, meticulously crafted down to the last tick.

Hierarchy is set by the instrument, not by decoration. The largest element is the field, then the
figure, then the guide; type is the smallest and the rarest, a sign-painter's name in a cut,
high-contrast hand and a few clinical figures in a plain sans. Nothing overlaps that should not;
every trim line lands exactly on a cut edge; every margin is the same as its twin. It should look
labored over for days by someone who has held a trimmer for decades and still checks the blade
before each pass.
