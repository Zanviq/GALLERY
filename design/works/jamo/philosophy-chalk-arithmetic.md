# Chalk Arithmetic

A letter is a sum. Every mark in this movement is built by addition and kept honest by subtraction:
one stroke more turns a hook into a key, one stroke less returns it. The visual world is a dark,
matte field on which pale, abrasive marks are added one at a time, and nothing is ever fully
removed. What is wiped leaves a ghost; what is written carries the tooth of the surface it was
dragged across. The work should look as if a patient hand made each line deliberately, and as if
someone at the top of their craft then decided which lines could stay.

Space is a slate, not a page. The field is one deep green-black plane with no frame and no edge
ornament; composition happens by placing a few marks against a great deal of quiet surface. Marks
sit on an implied ruled grid, square cells divided by a faint cross, the way a beginner's exercise
book divides the room for a single character. Alignment is strict and left-weighted, the grid is
never drawn louder than the marks it holds, and empty cells are allowed to stay empty. Every
interval is measured; the restraint is meticulously crafted, never accidental.

Colour is a set of chalks, each with one duty. White builds. Pale blue distinguishes the second
kind of sign from the first. Yellow is the hand acting now: the stroke just added, the thing being
held. Pink is judgement, used once, in a loop drawn around what is correct. No colour appears
where it has no job, and no colour is blended into another. The palette is calibrated with
painstaking attention so that each chalk keeps its contrast against the slate without glowing.

Material is the point. A chalk line is not a vector: it breaks where the slate's grain lifts, it
thickens where the hand presses, it sheds a little dust below itself. Those properties are
simulated stroke by stroke, procedurally, so no two marks are the same, but the simulation is held
back to the level of a real stick on a real board. Texture lives only inside marks and in the
residue of erasure; the ground itself stays clean. This is master-level execution of something
humble, and it must read as observed, not as an effect.

Hierarchy comes from scale and sequence, never from decoration. One large character carries a
composition; a family of characters, ordered from fewest strokes to most, makes a diagram; a small
annotation in the same hand anchors it. Text is rare and short, written in the same chalk as the
marks, and positioned with the precision of a draughtsman. The finished piece should feel like a
plate from an imaginary grammar of strokes: dense with care, sparse in words, and the product of
countless small decisions made by someone who knew exactly when to stop.
