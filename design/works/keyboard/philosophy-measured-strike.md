# Measured Strike

*A visual philosophy for 가락 GARAK — written before the brand mark, the share image and the page's technical drawings were made.*

A keystroke is the smallest unit of labour a hand performs on a machine, and this movement treats it with the seriousness of a surveyor. Every image begins as a measurement: a travel distance, a force, an interval between two beats. Form is not invented; it is plotted. The composition is a record of something that already happened under a fingertip, laid out on a cold aluminium-grey field the way an engineer pins a drawing to a board. The work must look as though it was measured twice and cut once, the product of painstaking calibration rather than inspiration.

Space is organised like a cross-section. Large quiet planes of mineral grey hold a single dense object, and everything else is reduced to hairlines, dimension ticks and small numerals that sit exactly where they belong. Nothing floats without a reason; each label has a leader line, each line ends in a point, and each point lands on an edge that exists. Asymmetry is deliberate: the weight of the object sits low and to one side, the type sits high and to the other, and the empty corner between them is part of the drawing. Master-level restraint means the empty corner is the hardest part to leave alone.

Colour comes only from material. Graphite, porcelain white, celadon and lacquer red are case colours before they are palette entries; brass is the one warm metal and is used the way a jeweller uses it, as a single inlay. A single accent — always the colour of the escape key — is allowed to touch the page, and it is never spread thin. There are no gradients for their own sake, no glow; light is what falls on chamfered metal, rendered with the patience of someone who has studied how anodised aluminium actually catches a window.

Rhythm is the soul of the movement. A row of three keycaps with the middle one pressed is a measure of music and a mechanical fact at once; the mark repeats that idea at every scale. Repetition is welcome — sixty-eight keys, eight layers, four colourways — but every repetition carries a small, exact variation, like a row profile changing its height by a millimetre. The result should feel meticulously crafted: dense where the object is, silent everywhere else.

Typography is engineered rather than decorated. Wide, upright Latin capitals behave like engraved legends; Korean set heavy and tight carries the voice; small numerals are tabular and aligned to the same baseline as the hairlines they annotate. Text is sparse and always earns its place as a label on the drawing — never a paragraph explaining what the drawing already shows. Every alignment is the outcome of countless small corrections, invisible once correct.
